Risk Radar · scope statement and product requirements · 11 September 2026

Risk Radar Blueprint

What we are building, in six parts. What each part is for, what it must do, and the number that tells us whether it is doing it.

Scope statement. Risk Radar watches a Nigerian bank's stream of payments, spots the ones that look like fraud, and puts them in front of a fraud analyst with a clear recommendation of what to do next. It advises; a person decides. It never blocks, holds or reverses a payment.

It is built from six parts: realistic data to learn from, a checklist of written rules for fraud that is obvious, a trained model for fraud that is not, a recommendation engine that turns the evidence into a next step, a case system with a permanent record, and a dashboard the fraud department works in all day.

How the parts connect

1
Part one

Data foundation — a realistic financial dataset, built with context

Objective: give the rules and the model believable Nigerian payment behaviour to learn from, with fraud that hides among ordinary life rather than standing apart from it.

No bank's real transactions are available, and no public Nigerian fraud dataset exists. So we generate customers — ordinary people, salaried workers, shop owners, traders — who each own accounts, keep habits and pay people, then have some of them robbed in three different ways. Real published statistics shape the result: our fraud rate is tuned to the one Nigeria's interbank settlement body reports.

Must do

  • Model customers owning several accounts, so one person's incident stays one investigation.
  • Give legitimate customers the behaviours fraud also has — paying new people, paying many people in an hour, occasional large sums — so fraud has something to hide in.
  • Fail its own check if any single measurement can separate fraud from normal by itself.

Part two

Rules checklist — fraud that is obvious

Objective: catch the fraud a person would recognise instantly, with near-certain accuracy, and catch it even when it is a new variant the model has never seen.

Plain written rules, each producing a named warning with its evidence — never a score. Some raise the risk (a burst of payments, a run of refused card attempts). Some override everything (a sanctioned destination). Some lower it (a long-standing payee receiving a normal amount). Settings change from the admin screen, with every change recorded.

Must do

  • Explain itself: every warning says what fired and why.
  • Stay inside the team's capacity: no rule may spend more of the daily alert budget than it earns.
  • Be tested one rule at a time, and drop any rule the data does not support.

3
Part three

Trained model — fraud that is complex, calibrated to tell the truth

Objective: catch fraud that no single rule describes — the combination of a new device, a quiet pause and a rush of transfers — and give a probability that means exactly what it says.

Hundreds of small decision trees, each correcting the last, trained on older data and then adjusted on newer data it never saw, so that "0.30" really means about three in ten. It is one voice, not the decision: on its own it catches only what resembles its training, which is why the rules sit beside it.

Must do

  • Be tested on a kind of fraud hidden from its training, not only on more of the same.
  • Produce an honest probability, because the daily workload is predicted from it.
  • Use the same twelve measurements live as in training, checked by a test.

4
Part four

Recommendation engine — the next step, in the bank's own procedure

Objective: turn the evidence into one clear next action and its reason — "block the card and reissue: 14 refused small attempts" — so an analyst never has to work out what to do from raw data.

It reads the verdict, the warnings, the money at risk and the customer's pattern, and picks the step from a playbook. It is deterministic and written down, never learned, so every recommendation can be explained in the words that produced it.

On "real bank policy". No bank has shared its fraud procedures with us, so the playbook today is our own, written from how Nigerian fraud desks describe their work. It is built as a lookup the bank can replace: their procedure in, their steps out, with no code change. We will not describe it as a bank's policy until one is loaded.

Must do

  • Give one action, numbered next steps, and the reason, for every case.
  • Say how urgent it is, tied to a response clock.
  • Record whether the analyst followed it, so the advice can be checked against real outcomes.

Part five

Case system and permanent record — what holds it together

Objective: turn many alerts about one customer into one case, keep the team inside its capacity, and prove afterwards who did what.

  • Group alerts about the same customer into one case, so an attacker moving between someone's accounts stays one investigation.
  • Hold the whole system to the daily alert budget — rules first, the model gets what is left.
  • Record every decision and action permanently; administrators may tune the system but can never open a case.

6
Part six

Dashboard — where the department works

Objective: let each person in the fraud department do their day's job from one screen: analysts clear cases, the lead sees whether the desk is coping, security handles break-ins, the administrator tunes settings.

Fraud analyst

  • The next case handed to them, largest risk first
  • Money at risk, the recommendation, why it fired
  • One key to record the outcome

Fraud operations lead

  • Backlog, waiting times, who carries what
  • How many alerts turned out to be false
  • Signs off analysts' outcomes

Information security

  • Cases showing a break-in — new device, dormant account
  • Escalations routed to them

Administrator

  • Rules, thresholds, models, the record
  • No access to any case, by design

The number that sums it up

Shown a kind of fraud hidden from its training, with the team held to 120 alerts a day, the whole system catches about eight incidents in ten (0.81): account takeover 0.78, mule fan-out 0.65, card testing 0.99. The model is what catches account takeover; the rules are what catch card testing; mule fan-out needs both.

We can claim the method is sound and measured. We cannot yet claim it works on real criminals, because we have never seen any. Running it alongside a real bank for a month, alerting nobody, is what would settle that.

Out of scope

  • Blocking, holding or reversing payments — advisory only, permanently.
  • Connecting to a real bank's systems — a separate project.
  • Detecting networks of linked criminals — a later version.
  • Testing a new model on live traffic before switching to it — designed, not built.
