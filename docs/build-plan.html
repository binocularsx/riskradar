What a bank-grade build actually requires — and the nine packages that get us there

The enterprise base PRD was treated as a checklist to score against. It is not a checklist. It is a description of a different shape of system, and three of its requirements do not add features to Risk Radar — they change the spine.

Benchmarks: Feedzai, IBM (2026) Regulation verified: CBN, Nacha 9 work packages · 6 built Supersedes base-prd-alignment.md §5.2, §7.1

First: the false alarms are not the problem. The unit is.

Reporting false positives per rule is not an admission — it is the single question the base PRD says ends vendor conversations, and best-in-class banks publish it. But we have been reporting it in the wrong unit. Banks do not benchmark counts of false alarms. They benchmark the ratio of false alerts to confirmed fraud, and the share of fraud value stopped. Measured that way, here is where Risk Radar actually sits.

The defensible line

Every one of those rows is a business choice, not a technical limit — and being able to show the choice is the thing. IBM's 2026 position is that operational capacity, not model quality, is now the binding constraint in fraud detection, and that the right question is not "how many alerts can we handle" but "how much fraud are we willing to miss." Risk Radar derives its thresholds from a fixed analyst capacity and reports recall at each. That is the architecture that question requires, and it was built in week three.

Two corrections to our current documents

The BVN watch-list is no longer a future requirement. CBN's addendum to the BVN framework was issued 12 March 2026 and took effect 1 May 2026. REG-NG-04 is live regulation today, not a roadmap item — a temporary flag of at most 24 hours, during which the bank must contact the customer for clarification.

There is a liability clause that speaks directly to D7. The APP framework puts the cost on the institution where it "fails to flag or freeze" a fraudulent transaction because its systems were inadequate, and lets CBN direct NIBSS to withhold settlement — extending to second-level beneficiary institutions. This does not mean Risk Radar should start blocking payments; D7 and D69a stand. It means the advisory output has to become something a bank can prove it received in time and acted on. That is WP-07, and it is the difference between "we chose not to enforce" and "we cannot be deployed into the liability regime that exists."

One more datum for the receiving-side argument: from March and June 2026, Nacha requires US receiving institutions to monitor incoming credits for the first time. Screening money coming in stopped being a differentiator and became a baseline obligation in two regimes at once, inside twelve months.

Third: what actually breaks in the architecture

These are not features we skipped. Each one is a structural assumption in the current spine that the industry standard invalidates — and each is cheaper to change now than after another two weeks of features land on top of it.

The event is a transaction

§6.1 defines a canonical transaction event. A login, a device binding, an MFA change, a SIM swap and a new-payee enrolment are none of those, and cannot be expressed in it at all. Every published account-takeover playbook is built on exactly these pre-authorisation signals.

payment-only schema → event envelope, payment as one type

Every profile is one-sided

Account, customer and destination profiles are all built from the sender's side. Receive-side mule detection needs the opposite: account age at the moment of credit, inbound credit velocity, and how fast money leaves again. Those features exist only in the receiving institution.

sender aggregates → two-sided profile store

Decisions are one-shot

Each transaction is scored alone. The receive-side playbook's core move is to link the inbound credit to the account's next outbound debit — a hold placed with conviction on the onward payment recovers more money than a millisecond decision on the credit.

one event, one decision → linked two-leg decision

Decisions have no expiry and no clock

ALLOW / MONITOR / REVIEW / HOLD carry no time-to-live and no regulatory deadline. Every Nigerian obligation above is a clock, and a 24-hour BVN flag is a decision that must expire on its own. A case has no field for when the customer first reported.

advisory band → directive with TTL + clock engine

The alert budget is one undifferentiated pool

All 120 alerts a day are routed to a human. The regulation itself names a machine action — flag the BVN for 24 hours and contact the customer — that no analyst needs to perform. Capacity should be allocated by disposition, not spent uniformly.

one queue → tiered disposition, capacity per tier

Nothing leaves the building

The hashed identifier was designed to travel (D9d), but there is no path for it to travel on. The 30-minute counterparty notification is an outbound obligation, and cross-bank signal exchange is where receive-side detection gets most of its lift.

closed system → counterparty channel, provable and timed

The target spine

Everything in plain outline exists and is tested. Everything in blue is new work, and carries the number of the package that builds it. The shape is unchanged — channel and switch in, point-in-time features, rules and model scored separately, immutable decision, correlated case, sealed audit. What changes is what an event can be, whose side the profiles are built from, and what comes out of the decision.

The nine work packages

Each is named for what it does, not for the component it adds. Dependencies run downward: nothing below needs anything above it that has not shipped.

WP-01

One event, not one transaction

Generalise the canonical transaction event into an envelope carrying an event_type, with the payment fields becoming one payload among several. Ingestion, validation and the feature package all key off the type. Nothing about payment scoring changes.

Why now: every other detection package is blocked behind it, and the cost of changing the event shape doubles with every feature written against the old one. This is the same argument §6.1 already makes for auth_result — and it was right then.

WP-02

Signals before the money moves

Consume the six events that precede a fraudulent transfer — login, failed-login burst, device binding, credential or MFA change, new payee enrolment, SIM change — and hold per-session state so a sequence can be scored, not just a payment.

Why now: our own evidence says the model catches account takeover and the rules barely touch it — and the strongest takeover signals are not transactions at all. Published takeover playbooks are built on precisely these pre-authorisation and in-session signals. The simulator can emit them; no bank required.

WP-03

Watch the money coming in

Build beneficiary-side profiles and score inbound credits: how old the receiving account was when the money landed, how many credits it has taken lately, and how fast the balance leaves again.

Why now: this stopped being optional in two regimes inside a year. Nacha made receiving-side monitoring mandatory in phases through 2026, and CBN's framework lets the regulator withhold settlement from second-level beneficiary institutions — meaning a Nigerian bank can now lose money on fraud it merely received.

WP-04

The second leg

Link an inbound credit to the receiving account's next outbound debit inside a watch window, and score the pair as one thing rather than two unrelated events.

Why now: the receive-side playbook's central finding is that the recoverable moment is the onward payment, not the credit — a short hold applied with conviction on the second leg recovers more than an instant decision on the first. Our mule fan-out typology is exactly this pattern seen from the wrong side.

WP-05

Clocks the regulator sets

A clock service: deadlines as versioned configuration rather than code, Nigerian working-day arithmetic, a first_reported_at on every case because that is what starts the refund clock, a counterparty institution field, and escalation when a clock is breached rather than a passive "past due" state.

Why now: all seven CBN obligations are clocks, and four of them are measured in working days — which is a calendar problem, not a timer problem. This is the largest regulatory gap we have, and it is entirely buildable without a bank because the clock engine is pure logic and pure tests.

WP-06

The twenty-four hour flag

A subject-level watch-list entry that expires on its own after 24 hours, prompts the analyst to contact the customer, and records the restriction and its lifting in the sealed audit chain.

Why now: CBN's BVN addendum took effect on 1 May 2026. This is not a roadmap item — it is current law, it is small, and it is the single cheapest way to show a Nigerian examiner that the build tracks live regulation rather than a textbook.

WP-07

A decision a bank can act on

Turn the advisory band into a contract: an idempotent directive with a time-to-live, a written fail-open policy, a shadow-mode switch, and a record of the moment the bank was told. Risk Radar still never enforces anything.

Why now: the APP framework places the loss on institutions whose systems fail to flag or freeze in time. We are not going to enforce — D7 stands — but the output has to be something a bank can wire in and prove it received. That turns "advisory by choice" into a deployable position instead of a limitation.

WP-08

Spend the budget by tier

The policy layer returns a disposition — close automatically, take the machine action, or send to a human — and capacity is allocated per tier instead of spending all 120 alerts on analyst review.

Why now: the regulation itself names an action no analyst is needed for (flag the subject, contact the customer). Routing by confidence is the one lever IBM identifies for scaling fraud operations without hiring, and our alert budget already has the calibrated probabilities that make tiering possible.

If only three get built

WP-09, then WP-05, then WP-01. The first changes how every number we already have is read. The second closes the largest regulatory gap on the board and is pure testable logic. The third unblocks everything else and only gets more expensive from here.

What we are deliberately not building

A plan is only credible if it says no to something. These stay out, and the reason is not time.

Blocking and enforcement

D7 stands. We build the seam and the proof of delivery; the bank enforces. Taking on automatic enforcement without a sponsor and a signed fail-open policy is a different project with a different liability.

One blended risk score

D11 stands. Blending an ordinal rule severity with a calibrated probability destroys the calibration the entire alert budget depends on — and the budget is our strongest architectural claim.

Graph and ring analysis

Real, and on the roadmap, but WP-04's linked second leg delivers most of the mule-ring value for a fraction of the work. Do the cheap version first and measure it.

Behavioural biometrics, identity bureaus

Both require data no simulator can honestly invent and no team can obtain without a bank. Claiming them on synthetic data would undo the one thing this project has been strict about.
