Ire's Deliverables

What Ire owns, what is finished, what is still to do, and the number that says each item is done.

Ire owns the data foundation and the evidence for detection: the simulator that generates the bank's customers and its fraud, the twenty-six measurements taken on every payment, the sign-in and credit events behind them, the trained model, and every evaluation that says how well the rules and the model work. Chidera wires these into the live system; Ire's job is to make sure what goes in is realistic and what comes out is measured honestly.

"Never seen in training" means that kind of fraud was hidden from the model while it learned, with the team held to 75 alerts a day (D76). Average across the seven: 0.822 (decision D82b); across the first three before the new types were added it was 0.960 (D79a), and those three did not fall. On its own the model scores 0.627 across the seven; it is useful alongside the rules, not instead of them, and must always be described that way. Finished — Ire presents these at the defence

  • The simulator. Customers of four kinds (ordinary, salary earner, small business, trader) who own one to three accounts, keep habits, pay known and new people. Three kinds of fraud generated as criminal processes, not as rule thresholds: account takeover, mule fan-out, card testing. Identities now come from the seed, so the same seed rebuilds the same bank (D68).
  • Four Nigerian fraud types (D82): scams paid into a collection account, SIM swap drains, dormant accounts, cloned cards, each with a legitimate twin (group collections, SIM replacements, owners returning, travel), four new measurements, and rule settings chosen by a stated criterion. The tuning caught three faults before anything shipped.
  • The dataset evaluator (D81): any transaction file mapped and measured through every layer at the budget, with the unseen-type test and a capability report; run on PaySim and two Nigerian files, with a label diagnostic proving those files unusable for detection. IEEE-CIS is mapped and ready.
  • The twenty-six measurements, one shared package for training and live, with a test proving they match. Version 1.4.1 adds region novelty, card-present counts, other senders to a destination and the local hour to version 1.3.0: five read sign-in, device, SIM and payee events (D77), five read money coming in (D78); "does not apply" is marked −1, ratios are capped at 100 (D64). The model reads seventeen: giving it the five receiving-side measurements cut unseen takeover from 0.991 to 0.857, so they stay with the rules (D78b).
  • The event and credit layers in the simulator: sign-in failures, device binding, credential and SIM changes, payee additions, and inbound credits with mule rings, each on its own random stream so the payments stay byte-identical (D77c, D78d). The corpus is now anchored to a date, so it rebuilds exactly.
  • The three new detectors, each tuned and measured before going live: account takeover sequence (D77b), inbound fan-in on a month of history (D78c), and the second leg, which took mule-ring value flagged from 63.5% to 84.9% (D79a).
  • The budget menu and the tier study: detection, false alerts and fraud value at every budget on a time-ordered test (D70a), and the measurement that machine actions take 24 alerts a day off the analysts at precision 0.992 with detection unchanged; refilling and auto-close were tested and rejected (D80a, D80b).
  • The calibrated model: gradient-boosted trees, trained on older data and adjusted on newer data it never saw, so its probability means what it says (D60–D61).
  • The held-out evaluation: six approaches compared on identical data at the same alert budget, with 95% ranges on every figure (D59, D63, D65, D67a).
  • The self-audit that caught eight "giveaway" measurements in our own data and led to the realistic population (D57), and the reproduction of the earlier dataset's result (D56).
  • PaySim validation: 65% of fraud value caught while catching 18% of fraud transactions; six of the original twelve measurements cannot fire on PaySim, and none of the ten event and credit measurements can, which we state rather than hide.

Still to do

1 Check "normal" against published Nigerian figures not started

Why
The most serious objection to the project is that our normal customers are invented. This turns "we made it up" into "we made it up and checked its shape".
What
Take published aggregates (NIBSS annual fraud reports, Central Bank payment statistics) and compare the simulator's shares side by side: channel mix, average transfer value, interbank (NIP) versus in-bank share, fraud loss by channel.
Done when
A table of our share against the published share for each; any gap wider than a third of the published value is either corrected in the simulator or explained in writing.

2 Run the real-label validation (IEEE-CIS) mapped · waiting for the files

Why
The only test short of a real bank with genuine fraud outcomes (about 590,000 transactions, 3.5% fraud).
What
Sign in to Kaggle, complete phone verification, accept the competition rules at kaggle.com/competitions/ieee-fraud-detection/rules, put train_transaction.csv in ml/data/public/, then run python ml/validate_public.py --dataset ieee. The script is written and ready.
Done when
Results file written; precision-recall area, calibration error and recall at the alert budget reported, with the caveat that it checks method, not our measurements (its columns are anonymised).

3 Measure the last three rules one at a time 10 of 13 done

Why
The Blueprint requires every rule to be tested on its own and dropped if the data does not support it. Card testing (right 99.8% of the time), the payment-burst rule (D67), the familiar-payee rule (D64), and the three added since (D77b, D78c, D79) are done.
What
For "saved payee" (lowers risk): how often it fires on fraud, which would hide it. For "sanctioned destination" and "known mule destination" (override everything): seed test lists and confirm they fire on every listed destination and nowhere else.
Done when
Each of the three has a firing rate, a share of fraud it wrongly lowers or correctly catches, and a keep-or-drop recommendation logged in the decision log.

4 Give the demo bank a longer memory done · burst rule 37/day

Why
The payment-burst rule now fires only on payments to a destination new to the bank. The demo bank remembers about 3 days, so a third of its payments look "new" and the rule fires about 50 times a day, 5 of them on fraud; in the 30-day training data it fires about 34 times a day (D67d).
What
Lengthen the demo profile's history (in simulator/riskradar_sim/generate.py) from 4 days towards 21–30, and measure how long the demo rebuild then takes. Done: 30 days (D76c). Rebuilt on 15 September: 414,239 history transactions (payments and credits) took 1 h 47 min to load and 34 min to score. In the live day the burst rule raised 37 alerts, just over the 35 aimed for; the whole system raised 72 against 75 and caught all three incidents. The receiving-side rules need the history's second half to settle (D78f).
Done when
On a rebuilt demo the rule fires at most about 35 times a day, the whole system stays within its alert budget (now 75 a day), and the rebuild time is written in the README.

5 Support the team's alert-budget decision done · 75 a day

Why
With the new rule, a smaller budget cuts false alarms sharply: 120 a day gives about 75 false alarms, 75 a day about 33, 60 a day about 20. The cost is account takeover never seen before: 0.777 at 120, 0.703 at 75, 0.658 at 60 (D67c).
What
Walk the team through the trade-off; once they choose, re-run ml/evaluate_system.py at the chosen budget and confirm the figures quoted in the Blueprint.
Done when
The team's choice is logged as a decision and every document quotes the figures for that budget. Done: 75 a day (D76); the held-out figures above are at 75.

Rules Ire works under

  • Detection never reads the simulator. Rules and model see only the payment and its measurements, never a generator setting; a test enforces this.
  • Publish the held-out number, never the flattering one from fraud the model has already seen.
  • Count incidents, not transactions, and never report accuracy: at 0.3% fraud, "never fraud" scores 99.7%.
  • Never train on someone else's column named risk, score, rate or fraud unless the code that made it can be read (D10f).
  • Log every change to what the numbers mean in the decision log, with its evidence, before quoting it.
